## Overview

**Scandinavia** is the centralized northern maritime state uniting the historic Danish, Swedish, and Norwegian realms. It controls the approaches between the North Sea and Baltic, retains the North Atlantic chain through Iceland and Greenland, and reaches North America through [Vinland](vinland.md).

The state suffers two major defeats against [Novgorod](novgorod.md) in the sixteenth century ATL. It loses part of Finland in the 1520s and then the rest of Finland, Estonia, and the Livonian territories in the 1550s. [Poland–Lithuania](poland-lithuania.md) joins the later war and receives Riga, Curonia, and southern Livonia.

These defeats do **not** break the union. Denmark never secedes. Instead, the loss of distant eastern territories and the need for more effective defense drive Scandinavia toward greater centralization. By 1604 it is smaller than the old union, but administratively and militarily more coherent.

## The constituent northern realms

Scandinavia grows from several historic kingdoms and regional systems:

- **Denmark**, controlling Jutland, the Øresund approaches, Skåneland, Schleswig, and Mecklenburg;
- **Sweden**, forming much of the central and eastern Scandinavian core after the loss of Finland;
- **Norway**, connecting the state to the North Atlantic, Iceland, Greenland, and Vinland;
- and associated islands, ports, frontier districts, and overseas territories.

Denmark, Sweden, and Norway continue to matter as historic identities and administrative regions, but they are no longer near-independent partners by 1604. Their exact surviving laws, estates, titles, and regional privileges remain **TBD**.

## Government and postwar centralization

### Before the northern wars

The earlier Scandinavian or Kalmar-style union is a looser dynastic and political structure. Separate regional administrations, military establishments, and elite interests make coordinated war difficult.

### The centralizing settlement

Defeat in the 1550s produces the opposite result from earlier drafts of the setting. Instead of Danish secession, the state undertakes central reform because:

- there is less territory to administer after the loss of Finland and the eastern Baltic;
- divided command is blamed for military failure;
- the Novgorodian and Poland–Lithuanian threat remains immediate;
- Denmark's money, population, fleet, and control of the Øresund are indispensable;
- and the North Atlantic possessions require common naval and commercial policy.

By 1604, Scandinavia possesses:

- one foreign policy;
- unified army and naval command;
- more standardized taxation and conscription;
- centralized fortress and naval planning;
- and a stronger common Scandinavian state identity.

The exact monarch, ministries, representative institutions, succession rules, and regional autonomy remain **TBD**.

## Territory

### European core

Current canon places within Scandinavia:

- Denmark and Jutland;
- Norway;
- Sweden;
- Skåneland and Malmö;
- Schleswig;
- Mecklenburg;
- and the major Danish and Scandinavian islands, subject to final mapping.

The exact German frontier, status of Holstein, and relationship between Mecklenburg and the wider HRE system remain **TBD**.

### North Atlantic system

Scandinavia controls or governs the northern chain of:

- Iceland;
- Greenland;
- and [Vinland](vinland.md).

The precise legal status of each territory remains unresolved. Vinland is a substantial North American territorial power rather than a minor outpost.

### Lost eastern territories

Scandinavia no longer controls:

- Finland;
- Estonia;
- Riga;
- Curonia;
- or Livonia.

Novgorod holds Finland, Estonia, and northern/eastern Livonia. Poland–Lithuania holds Riga, Curonia, and southern Livonia.

The status of Åland, Gotland, and other strategic islands remains **TBD**.

## Religion and society

Denmark becomes the first major Scandinavian kingdom to Christianize around **1050 ATL**, with wider Norwegian and Swedish conversion following during the 1100s. By 1604, Christianity is established throughout the state.

The exact denomination, relationship to the later Great Schism, church administration, surviving local rites, and religious minorities remain **TBD**.

Postwar centralization strengthens a common identity based on:

- memory of the lost eastern territories;
- common military service;
- maritime trade and naval culture;
- the Øresund and Baltic passages;
- and the North Atlantic colonial chain.

Regional Danish, Swedish, Norwegian, Icelandic, Greenlandic, and Vinlandic identities remain important even as state institutions centralize.

## Economy and infrastructure

Scandinavia sits across several strategic systems:

- the Øresund passage;
- North Sea commerce;
- Baltic trade;
- North Atlantic fisheries and shipping;
- Iceland–Greenland–Vinland routes;
- and coastal links to the HRE, Francia, Britain, and Novgorod.

Denmark's territories provide chokepoint leverage, agriculture, ports, and access to continental Europe. Sweden contributes the central Scandinavian land base and mineral potential. Norway and the Atlantic possessions supply maritime reach.

By 1604, centralization allows the government to coordinate:

- customs and passage policy;
- naval construction;
- rail and telegraph systems;
- coastal fortification;
- conscription;
- and overseas supply.

The exact Øresund dues regime, industrial centers, rail map, companies, currency, and colonial administration remain **TBD**.

## Armed forces

Scandinavia's military identity is shaped by defeat and reform.

### Navy

The navy is essential for:

- defending Denmark and the islands;
- controlling the Øresund;
- protecting North Atlantic communications;
- supporting Vinland;
- and preventing Novgorod from converting land victories into complete northern collapse.

The 1550s war demonstrates that Scandinavian naval power can preserve the core state even when its armies lose the eastern Baltic.

### Army

The postwar army becomes more centralized through:

- common command;
- standardized conscription;
- unified supply and artillery planning;
- and fortress systems protecting the reduced frontier.

Exact force size, weapons, mobilization plans, naval classes, and frontier fortresses remain **TBD**.

### Strategic doctrine

By 1604, Scandinavia is likely defensive and revisionist at the same time. It must protect a compact European core and dispersed Atlantic possessions while preparing for the possibility of recovering lost influence in Finland or the Baltics.

The final Great War plan remains **TBD**.

## Foreign relations

### Novgorod

Novgorod is the principal eastern rival. It holds Finland, Estonia, and northern/eastern Livonia, giving it a permanent Baltic position. Scandinavia regards these losses as a central national grievance.

### Poland–Lithuania

Poland–Lithuania's intervention in the 1550s transforms the war from a bilateral conflict into a coalition defeat. Its possession of Riga, Curonia, and southern Livonia creates another revisionist frontier.

### Vinland and the North Atlantic

[Vinland](vinland.md) connects Scandinavian power directly to North America. Its alliance with the [Iroquois Confederacy](iroquois-confederacy.md) and trade with the [UTA](united-tribes-of-america.md) give Scandinavia influence within the continental balance.

### HRE, Francia, and Britain

Scandinavia's control of northern passages and Mecklenburg makes relations with the HRE and Francia strategically important. Its role in the divided British system and the exact alignment in the coming Great War remain **TBD**.

## History

### Petty realms and union

The northern world begins as separate Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, and local realms. Over time they cohere into a Kalmar-style union linking the Baltic and North Atlantic systems.

The exact formation date and constitutional stages remain **TBD**.

### Christianization

Denmark Christianizes around 1050 ATL. Norway and Sweden follow in the 1100s, producing an established Christian northern world.

### Atlantic expansion

Scandinavian settlement and trade link the European north to Iceland, Greenland, and Vinland. Exact voyages, settlements, and colonial institutions remain unresolved.

### First Novgorodian defeat

In the 1520s, Novgorod takes part of Finland. Scandinavia remains united but faces a growing eastern threat.

### Baltic coalition war

In the 1550s, Novgorod and Poland–Lithuania attack on complementary fronts. Scandinavia loses the rest of Finland and the eastern Baltic territories.

### Centralization after defeat

The defeat becomes the founding crisis of the modern centralized state. Rather than dissolve, Scandinavia unifies taxation, command, naval planning, and foreign policy. By 1604, it is territorially reduced but institutionally stronger.

## Retired lore

The earlier V3 framework in which **Denmark secedes from the Scandinavian Union in the 1550s is retired**. Denmark remains part of Scandinavia through 1604, and the postwar state becomes more centralized rather than more fragmented.
