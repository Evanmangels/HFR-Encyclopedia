## Overview

The **United States**, usually abbreviated **USA**, is the principal Atlantic Roman-successor state of eastern North America and one of the continent's leading industrial powers. Descended from Roman colonies established along the Atlantic seaboard, the federation survived the collapse of Western Rome, expanded from a defensive coastal union into a continental empire, and for centuries regarded the extension of Roman-American political order across North America as both a strategic goal and a civilizational mission.

The USA is not a direct analogue of the OTL United States. Its dominant language is [Atlantic Standard](../languages/atlantic-standard.md), its civic traditions descend from Roman colonial municipalities and successor institutions, and its national mythology centers on the belief that Roman civilization survived and was renewed across the Atlantic. Christianity became increasingly intertwined with that identity as political and cultural influence shifted toward the northeastern and Atlantic states.

For centuries, territorial expansion appeared to confirm the USA's belief that the political future of the continent would eventually belong to it. That assumption collapsed during the [**First Great Indian War**](../history/events/first-great-indian-war.md), when the Mexica Double Eagle Empire, Iroquois Confederacy, United Tribes of America, and widespread Indigenous revolts shattered American control across much of the continent. Only a few years later, the [**Legionary Civil War**](../history/events/legionary-civil-war.md) separated the pagan Gulf and interior south as [Caesar's Legions](caesars-legions.md).

By **1604 ATL**, the USA remains populous, industrialized, militarily formidable, and politically ambitious. It is also a wounded state whose dominant political culture increasingly interprets the disasters of the late sixteenth century not as proof that continental expansion was impossible, but as proof that the federation had been too divided, decentralized, and politically weak to complete its historical mission.

This mood is commonly described in project terms as **wounded restorationism**.

## Name and terminology

**United States** and **USA** are the established project names. The final official Atlantic Standard endonym, constitutional title, demonym, and adjectival form remain **TBD**.

The state should be understood as an Atlantic Roman successor rather than a transplanted OTL Anglo-American republic. Its political vocabulary, legal inheritance, urban traditions, language, religious history, and conception of continental expansion all emerged from a radically different colonial and post-Roman history.

## History

### Roman colonies and the first federation

Roman exploration reached the Americas around the beginning of the fifth century ATL and eventually produced colonial settlements along the Caribbean, Gulf Coast, Atlantic seaboard, and parts of northern South America. After the western Roman state collapsed during the seventh century, its North American colonies did not disappear. Coastal cities, local courts, churches, militias, ports, road networks, and municipal governments allowed the colonies to survive as independent or semi-independent Roman-descended states.

The first durable Atlantic federation formed in **670 ATL**, when **Columbea** and **Nova Caesarea** united for mutual defense. The union was intended both to resist powerful Indigenous neighbors and to discourage attempts by Old World successor states to reclaim Rome's former overseas territories.

Its growth was gradual:

- **670** — Columbea and Nova Caesarea form the original federation;
- **700** — Boralea joins;
- **750** — Chesapeakae joins;
- **810** — Carolinea and Augustea join after stronger road connections tie them to the northern states;
- **c. 840** — Appalachia enters following a frontier dispute with Augustea;
- **c. 860** — Floria joins after federal intervention in a major Seminole conflict.

The early federation remained highly decentralized. Member states retained strong local identities and political institutions while pooling diplomacy, military resources, infrastructure, and trade.

The Gulf colonies followed a different path. **Texia, Missia, and Monterrae** formed **Liga Caesarea** around 820 ATL. Unlike the increasingly Christian Atlantic states, the Gulf had received large numbers of pagan migrants after Christianity became Rome's official religion, producing a distinct Gulf-Roman political, linguistic, and religious culture.

Liga Caesarea remained outside the federation for nearly two centuries before being coerced, pressured, or otherwise forced into union around **c. 1000 ATL**. The exact mechanism remains **TBD**, but the memory that the Gulf had not entered the federation entirely voluntarily later became central to Legionary separatism.

#### The Plains Mounted Confederation and Cahokia

During the wider era of North American state formation, a powerful mounted Indigenous confederation arose in the middle and upper Great Plains. Its final endonym, exact chronology, government, leadership, and relationship with the Sioux remain unresolved; the encyclopedia therefore uses **Plains Mounted Confederation** as a neutral working term rather than a final in-world name.

The confederation became a major military force of the continental interior and demonstrated that mounted Indigenous states could halt or reverse Roman-American expansion. It was closely associated with **Cahokia**, whether as conqueror, protector, inheritor, or political center; that relationship has not yet been finalized.

The confederation eventually disappeared, probably amid internal political crisis, but its memory endured among later Indigenous powers as evidence that American expansion was neither natural nor irreversible.

### Continental expansion

Over the following centuries, the USA expanded far beyond its Atlantic beginnings. The federal state increasingly developed an ideology of **continental destiny**, presenting westward expansion as the continuation of Rome's historical mission. Roads, cities, courts, Christianity, Atlantic Standard literacy, railways, industry, and federal law were portrayed as the instruments through which ordered civilization would be extended across North America.

From the perspective of many peoples subjected to this process, the same expansion meant conquest, coerced treaties, displacement, destruction of political autonomy, and forced incorporation into an increasingly powerful Roman-American state.

The USA fought **many wars during its centuries of continental expansion**. Only a small number of those wars have yet been canonized in detail. The absence of named articles for every conflict should not be read as evidence of a peaceful or uninterrupted advance: the continental expansion era included repeated campaigns, rebellions, treaties, frontier crises, state collapses, and wars whose final names and chronologies remain **TBD**.

#### The Razing of Cahokia

Among the most consequential events of the expansion era was the **Razing of Cahokia**.

Cahokia had survived as one of the most important sacred and political centers of the Indigenous interior and was deeply associated with the surviving Mound world. During American continental expansion, USA forces captured and deliberately destroyed the city with catastrophic loss of life. The exact date, campaign, and political authority controlling Cahokia at the moment of its destruction remain unresolved.

Its wider meaning is not.

The destruction of Cahokia marked the effective **end of the surviving Mound civilization as an independent political world**. What had once been a major center of continental civilization became both a symbol of American conquest and one of the great traumas of Indigenous historical memory.

For American expansionists, Cahokia could be remembered as a decisive victory over a powerful interior center. For many Indigenous peoples, it became proof of what complete American victory could mean. Its memory later informed the sacred and political importance attached to Cahokia during resistance to USA rule.

### The Great Indian War

The **First Great Indian War** was the decisive collapse of American continental supremacy.

By the later sixteenth century the USA had expanded across much of North America, reached the Pacific, subdued much of the Great Basin, and held California and extensive western territories. Many within the political elite increasingly assumed that remaining Indigenous powers could delay American expansion but could no longer reverse it.

The war destroyed that assumption.

The opening attacks came from the [**Mexica Double Eagle Empire**](mexica-double-eagle-empire.md) and the [**Iroquois Confederacy**](iroquois-confederacy.md), threatening two of the USA's most important strategic regions: the Gulf and lower Mississippi system, and the northeastern metropolitan-industrial core. Roughly a month later, the [**United Tribes of America**](united-tribes-of-america.md) entered the conflict while rebellions spread through the Great Plains, Great Basin, and other recently conquered territories.

The USA suddenly faced a continent-wide war. Mexica armies devastated American positions in Texia and Monterrae and campaigned across the southwest and Baja. Iroquois forces struck into Columbea and Nova Caesarea, threatened the northeastern heartland, and severed Boralea from the main American system. UTA forces encircled a major American army and drove toward the Mississippi basin.

Several American victories prevented total collapse. The UTA suffered a major defeat during the prolonged struggle for **Cahokia**. In the Gulf, American forces under the still-placeholder commander **Mr C** reversed the Mexica offensive in the battle later remembered as the **Miracle on the Bayou**. In the far west, the MDE failed to take [**New Vegas**](new-vegas.md), whose defense drew on surviving Californian forces and Indigenous mercenaries.

These victories saved the federation. They did not restore the old continental empire.

By the final phase, none of the major belligerents retained the manpower or material required for a decisive continental victory. Exhaustion produced stalemate and eventually peace. Strategically, however, the USA had lost. California was cut off and became the [**New California Republic**](new-california-republic.md); New Vegas emerged as an independent hinge state; the Great Basin fragmented into a mosaic of Indigenous powers; the UTA retained enormous territories in the interior; and the Iroquois and MDE survived as major powers capable of checking future American expansion.

The war's deepest consequence was psychological. Other powers learned that the USA could be defeated. Americans were forced to confront the collapse of an assumption that had guided centuries of policy: that every frontier was temporary.

For campaign detail, see the dedicated [First Great Indian War article](../history/events/first-great-indian-war.md).

### The Legionary Civil War

The Great Indian War was followed almost immediately by a deeper internal crisis.

The pagan Gulf and interior south had long resented northeastern political dominance, the growing Christian character of public life, federal centralization, and the memory of Liga Caesarea's coerced incorporation. During the Great Indian War, many Gulf communities also concluded that federal authorities had sacrificed southern security in order to prioritize the Christian, Atlantic Standard-speaking northeastern heartland.

In **1575 ATL**, these tensions erupted into the [**Legionary Civil War**](../history/events/legionary-civil-war.md). The secessionists formed [**Caesar's Legions**](caesars-legions.md), presenting themselves not merely as separatists but as heirs to a rival version of Roman civilization: Gulf-centered, pagan, martial, and hostile to what they regarded as the Christianized and increasingly centralized Atlantic federation.

The war continued until **1581** and was exceptionally destructive. The USA regained substantial territory and prevented the complete collapse of federal authority, but it failed to destroy the breakaway state. Caesar's Legions survived as an independent polity and became the USA's principal ideological and territorial rival.

The war therefore completed what the Great Indian War had begun. The federation had not only lost distant western conquests; its own Roman-American civilization had split into competing states.

For detailed campaigns and unresolved military questions, see the dedicated [Legionary Civil War article](../history/events/legionary-civil-war.md).

## Government and politics

The exact constitutional structure of the USA remains **TBD**.

Historically, the federation developed from a comparatively decentralized union of Roman-descended states, municipalities, courts, councils, and local political institutions. Defense, common infrastructure, diplomacy, and interregional commerce gradually strengthened federal authority, but regional autonomy remained a major feature of American political life for centuries.

Before the late-sixteenth-century crises, three broad political tendencies were especially important.

### Federal pluralists

Federal pluralists favored strong state autonomy, constitutional limits on central authority, and comparatively broad religious toleration.

### Centralist nationalists

Centralists favored stronger federal institutions, executive power, industrial modernization, standardized Atlantic Standard, a more explicitly Christian public identity, and greater national integration.

### Pagan and Gulf interests

Gulf political movements defended regional autonomy, pagan religious rights, and the historical privileges of the former Liga Caesarea states. They frequently found common cause with federal pluralists against northeastern centralization.

### The postwar political order

By 1604 the USA is best understood as lying between an **emergency republic** and a **proto-imperial republic**, though the exact constitutional terminology remains soft canon.

The country is no longer simply governed under temporary wartime emergency powers. Instead, some of the extraordinary authority created during the Great Indian War and Legionary Civil War appears to have survived the end of the immediate crises and become normalized within the ordinary state.

The constitution still exists. Elections still occur. State governments and representative institutions remain politically meaningful. Yet the political balance inside that constitutional order has changed sharply.

The secession of the heavily pagan Gulf removed one of the strongest electoral and institutional bases of federalist opposition. Centralists could therefore consolidate control through **legal elections and institutional politics** rather than through an overt military seizure of power. The process can be understood as a **soft constitutional coup**: legal in form, but transformative in effect.

A coalition previously constrained by Gulf, pagan, and federalist voting blocs became structurally dominant once those blocs largely disappeared from the remaining federation. Wartime emergency powers strengthened the executive, military administration, railway bureaucracy, and security apparatus; some of those powers were subsequently retained rather than fully surrendered.

By 1604, the USA remains a republic, but one in which republican institutions operate beneath a much stronger centralized state than existed before the two great wars.

The exact title, legal authority, selection process, and personality of the current national executive remain **TBD**.

## Religion and legitimacy

Christianity dominates public life in the modern USA.

This religious character has deep historical roots. While Rome remained officially pagan, Christian communities disproportionately migrated toward the Atlantic colonies. After Christianity became the Roman state religion in 520 ATL, the direction of religious migration reversed, with pagans concentrating more heavily in the Gulf colonies.

The result was a long-term cultural distinction between a more Christian-weighted Atlantic world and a more heavily pagan Gulf world. The USA nevertheless remained religiously plural for much of its history, especially after the incorporation of Liga Caesarea.

That pluralism weakened during the centuries of centralization and deteriorated sharply after the Legionary Civil War. By 1604, Christianity, Roman civic identity, Atlantic Standard literacy, patriotism, and loyalty to the federation are increasingly presented as mutually reinforcing features of respectable citizenship.

Pagans still live within the USA, but their political position has declined substantially. They are often suspected of sympathy with Caesar's Legions, and organized pagan political influence has largely disappeared from national government. The precise legal system of anti-pagan discrimination remains **TBD**; exact restrictions on temples, officeholding, schools, loyalty oaths, or public ritual should not yet be treated as locked canon.

The larger trajectory is clear: the older pluralistic federation is increasingly giving way to a Christian-defined federal nationalism.

## Language and culture

The principal language of the USA is **[Atlantic Standard](../languages/atlantic-standard.md)**, a Romance language descended from the Latin-speaking Atlantic colonial world.

Atlantic Standard is not merely a surviving colonial dialect. Over centuries, courts, education, administration, commerce, mass literacy, railways, and telegraph networks transformed it into one of the central instruments of federal integration.

The USA remains culturally diverse. Regional identities originated in the different Roman colonies and were later reshaped by Indigenous incorporation, migration, continental settlement, religious differentiation, Germanic migration, industrialization, and centuries of internal movement.

Despite this diversity, the modern state increasingly promotes a national culture centered on:

- Atlantic Standard;
- Roman civic inheritance;
- Christianity;
- federal loyalty;
- industrial modernity;
- military service;
- and the historical mission of continental civilization.

The Northeast exercises particularly strong influence over standardized language, higher education, industry, national politics, and public religious culture.

## Geography and regional identity

The remaining USA occupies much of the Atlantic and eastern interior of North America. Its current international boundaries are best understood through the [Interactive Globe](../world/interactive-globe.md), which reflects newer border work than several older prose summaries.

Several broad cultural and economic regions remain important.

### Northeast

The Northeast is the political, educational, financial, and increasingly ideological center of the federation. Its Christian-weighted political culture and influence over standardized Atlantic Standard have expanded markedly since the loss of the Gulf.

### Columbea and the Mid-Atlantic

One of the oldest, densest, and most urbanized regions of the country, with major ports, commercial networks, industry, and infrastructure.

### Southeast

An old Atlantic colonial region distinct from both the northeastern core and the now-seceded Gulf. Florida remains part of the USA despite the loss of the western Gulf system.

### Great Lakes

One of the country's principal industrial and strategic regions. It lies directly against the Iroquois and UTA systems and has become increasingly important to postwar military planning.

### Interior and frontier belt

Former frontier lands that survived the territorial collapse now form some of the country's most militarized regions. Railways, river crossings, fortified towns, and military depots increasingly define the political geography of these areas.

### Former territories and territorial claims

Modern American geography cannot be understood without recognizing how much territory lies outside the federation that was once controlled, occupied, or claimed by it.

Before the Great Indian War, the USA had expanded to the Pacific and controlled extensive portions of the western interior. Its former holdings now include territories controlled by or associated with:

- [Caesar's Legions](caesars-legions.md);
- the [United Tribes of America](united-tribes-of-america.md);
- the [Iroquois Confederacy](iroquois-confederacy.md);
- the [New California Republic](new-california-republic.md);
- [New Vegas](new-vegas.md);
- and the fragmented Great Basin.

The precise legal boundaries of modern American territorial claims remain incompletely defined. Not every territory formerly controlled by the federation is necessarily the subject of an identical formal claim, and older textual border summaries have been superseded in several places by current map work.

The broader restorationist principle, however, is important: **the USA does not emotionally regard the political map of 1604 as final**.

Caesar's Legions are widely regarded as rebel territory. NCR can be imagined as a lost branch of the same Roman-American civilization rather than an entirely foreign society. The Great Basin evokes an unfinished continental project, while UTA and Iroquois territory includes lands once subjected to some form of American rule, occupation, or claim.

This does not imply preparation for simultaneous reconquest of every former territory. It means that acceptance of the postwar map is often strategic rather than ideological.

## Economy and infrastructure

Despite its territorial losses, the USA remains one of North America's largest industrial economies.

Its strongest economic regions include the northeastern Atlantic coast, Columbea and the Mid-Atlantic, the southern Great Lakes, major Atlantic ports, and surviving portions of the Mississippi-connected economic system.

Major sectors include:

- steel and heavy machinery;
- railway equipment;
- arms and artillery;
- shipbuilding;
- mining and coal;
- textiles;
- grain processing;
- banking and insurance;
- and Atlantic commerce.

Infrastructure has always been central to American state power. The early federation depended on coastal roads, navigable rivers, ports, and inherited Roman infrastructure. Continental expansion later produced vast rail and telegraph networks intended to bind conquered territories to the Atlantic core.

The disasters of the late sixteenth century transformed that geography. Some railways that once served continental expansion now terminate at international borders. Others continue into successor states or politically fragmented territory. New military routes increasingly connect the Atlantic industrial core to the Great Lakes and fortified frontiers.

The infrastructure of the modern USA therefore carries the visible imprint of lost empire.

## Armed forces

The USA remains one of the strongest military-industrial states in North America.

Its armed forces were profoundly shaped by the Great Indian War, which exposed inadequate coordination among federal and regional forces, overextended continental logistics, weak frontier intelligence, vulnerability to highly mobile Indigenous armies, and the danger of simultaneous multi-front warfare.

Postwar military thinking increasingly emphasizes:

- rail mobilization;
- centralized command;
- fortified frontiers;
- machine guns;
- quick-firing artillery;
- modern military intelligence;
- telegraphic security;
- fortified bridges and river crossings;
- and doctrine designed to counter highly mobile cavalry forces.

The central strategic nightmare of the American military establishment is a second continental coalition capable of reproducing the strategic conditions of the Great Indian War.

Aviation remains experimental, as it does throughout most of the world in 1604 ATL. Railways, artillery, cavalry reconnaissance, telegraphy, river transport, and conventional ground observation therefore remain decisive.

## Internal security

The Great Indian War and Legionary Civil War transformed the relationship between the federal government and its own population.

Security institutions increasingly concern themselves with:

- Legionary espionage;
- pagan separatism;
- sabotage of railways and telegraphs;
- frontier Indigenous movements;
- foreign agents;
- political extremism;
- and groups suspected of divided loyalty.

The exact structure and legal authority of these institutions remain unresolved. Their broader political importance does not. The modern USA is considerably more centralized, suspicious, and security-conscious than the federation from which it developed.

## Foreign relations

### Caesar's Legions

[Caesar's Legions](caesars-legions.md) are the USA's principal enemy. The conflict is territorial, ideological, religious, and civilizational. Much of American political culture regards CL not as an ordinary foreign country but as the surviving product of rebellion and the occupation of territory that should ultimately belong within the federation.

Long-term restoration of federal authority over at least some Legionary territory remains central to American restorationist thought, even when immediate military circumstances make renewed war undesirable.

### Iroquois Confederacy

The [Iroquois Confederacy](iroquois-confederacy.md) is one of the greatest direct threats to the northeastern American heartland. Its survival as an independent modern Indigenous power directly challenges the old narrative of inevitable USA expansion. Its Great Lakes position and ties to Vinland make it especially important to American strategic planning.

### United Tribes of America

The [United Tribes of America](united-tribes-of-america.md) is the great rival of the upper interior. Its territorial gains during the Great Indian War remain a major source of humiliation. American planners particularly fear the UTA's combination of geographic depth, cavalry mobility, Indigenous political legitimacy, and access to northern Pacific and Vinlandic trade networks.

### Mexica Double Eagle Empire

The [Mexica Double Eagle Empire](mexica-double-eagle-empire.md) was one of the principal enemies of the Great Indian War. It no longer directly borders the USA because Caesar's Legions occupies the intervening southwestern system, but its power remains fundamental to the continental balance.

### Vinland

[Vinland](vinland.md) is both a northern Atlantic power and a conduit through which Scandinavian influence enters North American politics. Its relationship with the Iroquois makes it strategically significant even where the USA–Vinland relationship is not openly hostile.

### New California Republic

American attitudes toward the [New California Republic](new-california-republic.md) are more complicated than relations with the UTA or CL. NCR emerged because California was cut off from the eastern federation rather than through an initial anti-American secession. Many Americans can therefore view it as a lost branch of the same Roman-American civilization rather than as an inherently hostile foreign society.

Ideas of eventual reunion can coexist with diplomatic recognition and political rivalry.

### New Vegas

[New Vegas](new-vegas.md) is regarded with a mixture of distrust and reluctant respect. Its independence, wealth, strategic location, and willingness to trade across ideological lines make it valuable but politically unreliable from the American perspective.

## National identity

American national identity rests on several interconnected historical claims:

- the Roman colonies survived the collapse of the western empire;
- the federation preserved Roman civilization in the Americas;
- Roman political order was reborn across the Atlantic;
- and continental expansion represented the extension of that civilization rather than ordinary territorial conquest.

For most of American history, expansion appeared to prove those beliefs correct.

The late-sixteenth-century catastrophes created a crisis in which reality contradicted the national myth. The dominant postwar response has not been to abandon the myth, but to change the explanation for failure.

In centralist and restorationist political culture, the USA lost because it had been too decentralized, politically divided, insufficiently militarized, betrayed by pagan separatists, surprised by Indigenous coordination, and weakened by leaders who failed to act decisively.

This interpretation carries enormous political consequences. If the continental mission itself was wrong, then the Great Indian War demands restraint. If the mission was right but the state was too weak to accomplish it, then defeat demands **centralization**.

The latter interpretation increasingly dominates the politics of 1604.

## Wounded restorationism

The defining political mood of the modern USA is **wounded restorationism**.

The state remembers itself not as a former continental empire whose age has simply passed, but as a great civilization temporarily deprived of territory, strategic depth, and prestige.

Its enemies interpret the new North American order as proof that the continent is permanently multipolar.

The USA is not yet convinced.

Its political institutions, military planning, educational narratives, frontier infrastructure, and territorial imagination remain oriented toward the possibility that at least some portion of what was lost can eventually be recovered.

This does not necessarily imply an immediate war of reconquest. The federation has endured two enormous conflicts within a generation. It does mean that American acceptance of the present settlement is frequently pragmatic rather than emotional.

The borders of 1604 are treated as facts.

They are not universally treated as legitimate.

## Society

The USA is a culturally and ancestrally diverse Roman-American society. Its population includes descendants of multiple Roman colonial groups, later Old World migrants, Indigenous peoples incorporated during centuries of expansion, and frontier communities shaped by long-term regional movement.

The development of slavery, ancestry, and social hierarchy differs substantially from OTL because HFR did not produce the same large-scale trans-Atlantic African slave system. Social prejudice instead developed through a different combination of religion, language, ancestry, political loyalty, citizenship, frontier status, and association with hostile powers.

Christian, Atlantic Standard-speaking Indigenous communities incorporated into long-settled regions may participate in ordinary civic society, while peoples associated with the UTA, Iroquois, or recently lost frontier territories can face considerably greater suspicion.

The exact systems governing treaty populations, citizenship, local Indigenous autonomy, and internal frontier administration remain only partially developed and should not yet be standardized across the entire federation.

## The USA in 1604

The United States remains one of the great powers of North America.

Its armies are modern. Its factories remain productive. Its Atlantic ports remain wealthy. Its railways bind together one of the most industrialized regions of the hemisphere. Its government possesses greater administrative reach than at any earlier moment in its history.

Yet the state is territorially smaller and politically less secure than it was only a generation earlier.

The paradox defines modern America:

**the country became more powerful internally at precisely the moment its continental empire collapsed externally.**

The old federation responded to military disaster with centralization. The old pluralistic balance was broken by secession. Institutions created or strengthened during emergency survived into peacetime. National mythology hardened rather than disappeared.

The USA of 1604 is therefore not a dying state. It is a still-powerful civilization that remembers a much larger map, believes history once promised it continental supremacy, and has not yet decided how much of its older republican balance it is willing to sacrifice in order to recover what it believes was lost.
