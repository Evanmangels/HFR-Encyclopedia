## Overview

Around **400 ATL**, Roman pilots reach the Americas. Contact develops into a chain of colonial nodes in the Caribbean, along the Gulf Coast and Atlantic seaboard, and across parts of northern South America. These settlements do not initially resemble later mass-settler empires. They are maritime and coastal: ports, fortified stations, merchant enclaves, plantations, timber stations, islands, and settlements along navigable rivers.

This event transforms HFR more than almost any later conquest. The Americas become part of the Roman world before the western Empire collapses and before Christianity becomes the Roman state religion. As a result, colonial institutions and religious migrations develop inside a living late-Roman system rather than through a later OTL-style age of discovery.

## Discovery and early voyages

The exact route, expedition leader, departure port, ship type, and circumstances of first landfall are **TBD**. The canon establishes only that Roman maritime exploration reaches the Caribbean around 400 and that permanent colonization follows.

Maritime cartography and pilot guilds become important imperial assets. Mediterranean shipping networks begin to connect with westbound convoys, and Atlantic-facing ports in Iberia and the Maghreb gain new significance.

## Colonial geography

The first wave ultimately includes:

- the **Caribbean**, where island nodes preserve strong maritime continuity;
- the **Atlantic seaboard**, where colonies later contribute to the formation of the [United States](../../polities/united-states.md);
- the **Gulf Coast**, which later becomes the center of a distinctive pagan Roman successor culture;
- **northern South America**, especially the Colombian, Venezuelan, and Panamanian coastal world;
- coastal rivers and immediate hinterlands, rather than uninterrupted continental territorial control.

Exact colony rosters, founding dates, and imperial administrative divisions remain **TBD**.

## Indigenous contact and disease

Roman contact introduces Old World diseases and causes severe Indigenous mortality, but smallpox does not exist in HFR. The cumulative planning figure is roughly **50 percent mortality over time**, not the more totalizing collapse associated with many OTL models.

The Americas are therefore transformed but not emptied. Indigenous states and confederacies remain capable of military resistance, diplomacy, institutional adaptation, and later industrial development. This is one of the foundations of the world's long-term multipolarity.

## Religious migration

Because Rome remains officially pagan until 520, Christians move into parts of the Atlantic colonial world during the earlier period. After the [Christianization of the Roman Empire](christianization-of-rome.md), pressure reverses and pagan migrants increasingly concentrate in the Gulf colonies.

This creates the deep historical distinction between:

- a more Christian-weighted Atlantic seaboard;
- a more strongly pagan Gulf world;
- and a wider colonial sphere that remains culturally and politically plural.

## Collapse and colonial survival

When western Rome falls in the [600s ATL](fall-of-western-rome.md), most American colonial nodes become de facto independent rather than disappearing. Their later fates differ:

- Atlantic and Gulf colonies survive as separate successor states;
- Caribbean islands largely preserve local governments and maritime continuity;
- many South American colonies are destroyed or reduced to a small number of surviving cities;
- surviving legal, clerical, and municipal records provide institutional memory for later federations.

## Long-term significance

Roman colonization creates an **Atlantic Roman world**, not simply European colonies overseas. The USA, Liga Caesarea, Caesar's Legions, Caribbean states, and surviving South American cities all derive part of their institutional or cultural history from this first wave.
